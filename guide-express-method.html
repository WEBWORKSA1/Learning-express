The Express Method: How to Learn Any Skill Fast

A five-step, research-backed system for learning new skills quickly: define, deconstruct, practise, get feedback and review.

Most people do not learn slowly because they lack talent. They learn slowly because they practise the wrong things in the wrong order, and they rarely find out what they are getting wrong. The Express Method fixes that with five steps you can apply to coding, a language, an instrument or a professional exam.

1. Define the finish line

“Learn Spanish” is not a goal; “hold a 15-minute conversation about my work” is. A concrete target tells you which parts of a skill matter and which you can postpone. Write your finish line in one sentence and add a date. If you cannot describe what success looks like, you cannot plan a route to it.

2. Deconstruct the skill

Every skill is a bundle of sub-skills. Spreadsheet modelling breaks into formulas, lookups, pivot tables and charting. Public speaking breaks into structure, delivery and handling questions. List the sub-skills, then identify the 20% that will deliver most of the results for your finish line. That short list becomes your first set of “stations”.

Express tip: Look at ten real examples of the end result — ten job ads, ten conversations, ten finished projects — and note what appears repeatedly. Frequency is a reliable guide to priority.

3. Practise deliberately, not repetitively

Deliberate practice means working at the edge of your ability on a specific weakness, with full attention. Ten minutes on the one chord change you keep fumbling beats an hour of playing songs you already know. Keep sessions short and focused: 25–50 minutes, one target per session.

Use retrieval rather than review. Instead of re-reading notes, close them and reproduce the idea from memory. The struggle you feel is not a sign of failure — it is the mechanism that strengthens memory. Our active recall guide explains why.

4. Shorten the feedback loop

The faster you discover a mistake, the faster you correct it. Programmers get instant feedback from running code; language learners can get it from recording themselves and comparing with native speakers; writers can get it from a peer or a checklist. Design your practice so feedback arrives in minutes, not weeks.

  • Self-tests: quizzes and flashcards tell you immediately what you do not know.
  • Models: compare your work side by side with an expert example.
  • People: tutors, mentors and study partners catch errors you cannot see.

5. Space it out and come back

Learning that sticks is learning that is revisited. Schedule short reviews after one day, three days, a week and a month. Each review takes less time than the last, and the knowledge becomes durable. Our flashcard tool and 30-day planner build this rhythm in for you.

Putting it together: a 20-hour sprint

Many skills reach a useful beginner level in roughly 20 hours of focused practice. Split them across two or three weeks: one hour on weekdays, a longer session at the weekend, and a quick review each morning. Track hours on a simple log. When you hit 20, test yourself against the finish line you wrote in step one and decide whether to go deeper or move to the next skill.
